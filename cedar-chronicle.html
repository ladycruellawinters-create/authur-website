CULTURE & CONVERSATION

Book club seeks readers. Agreement optional.

Evelyn Greene and Daniel Blackwood make room for another evening of lively opinions.

By Elias Mercer

At Twilight Books, a comfortable chair is an invitation to stay. Add Evelyn Greene and a shared reading choice, and staying quiet becomes rather less likely.

The town’s monthly book club brings readers together after sunset, with Evelyn organising and Daniel hosting among the shelves. The appeal is simple: a book is something you can enjoy alone, then discover all over again through somebody else’s opinion.

New readers need not arrive with a speech prepared. Listening is welcome; so is changing your mind. The Chronicle cannot promise that anyone will agree about the ending.

Reading choices and meeting notices will be announced on the community board. Until then, there is plenty of time to practise the delicate art of letting somebody else finish a sentence.

LOCAL AIRWAVES

Who had the story first? Local media debate continues.

WCHL 88.3 and the Chronicle find common ground. Almost.

From the editor’s desk

Felix Bellomy has a gift for making a town-wide broadcast sound like a conversation across a kitchen table. This is an admirable quality. It does not, in this editor’s opinion, settle the question of where he found the subject of that conversation.

The WCHL 88.3 presenter maintains that he merely delivers local stories faster. The Chronicle maintains that there is something to be said for putting them in writing, where they can be examined at leisure and cannot be interrupted by Felix.

Fortunately, both publications agree that Cedar Hollow deserves to hear about its own residents. We also agree with the mayor that an unfinished festival arrangement is not yet an announcement.

Further developments will be reported here. Unless, of course, Felix gets there first.

BUSINESS ON MAIN STREET

Before you throw it away, ask Greta.

Gears & Grime makes a case for the useful thing in the bottom drawer.

Neighbourhood notebook

A loose part can look like clutter until it is precisely the part somebody needs. Greta Grindle, proprietor of Gears & Grime, has considerable sympathy for this point of view and rather less for throwing useful things away.

Resourcefulness is part of her trade. So is pride in a repair done properly. Anyone arriving with an apology for the state of an engine may find that the engine receives the longer conversation.

Arthur Bell’s work at The Ticking Time Peace calls for a different sort of patience, but the two share an appreciation for things that deserve another chance. Their friendly rivalry is a reminder that there is more than one way to be exacting.

The Chronicle offers no verdict on who wins an argument about mechanics. It is, however, willing to listen.

AROUND THE NEIGHBOURHOOD

This year’s pumpkins: the best yet?

Jack Crow’s annual confidence remains in excellent condition.

Autumn observations

For Jack Crow, autumn comes with work-worn hands, pumpkin-growing pride and a familiar prediction: this year’s harvest will be his best.

The produce farmer’s enthusiasm extends beyond the field. Elaborate carved lanterns and a fondness for autumn celebrations give him plenty of reasons to enjoy the season, even when the work is demanding.

There is something reassuring about a neighbour who greets the turning year with the same wholehearted optimism. The Chronicle is not equipped to grade pumpkins, but it is prepared to appreciate a good lantern.

No new festival date is announced in this report. The mayor may put her pen down.

PLACES TO LINGER

At the Slumbering Swan, quiet is part of the welcome.

Chamomile for the innkeeper. Excellent coffee for everyone else.

A look around town

There are establishments that compete for your attention. The Slumbering Swan Bed & Breakfast would appear to prefer that you take a seat.

Innkeeper Silas Sommers brings an old-fashioned manner, a soft voice and a wonderfully dry sense of humour to welcoming guests. He favours chamomile tea, keeps excellent coffee for visitors and has very little enthusiasm for televisions in the guest bedrooms.

Antique pillows and feather beds are among his interests. His ginger cat, Drowse, takes an equally committed approach to comfort, though with fewer conversational obligations.

For a town that can produce a great deal of conversation, a little quiet has its attractions. Felix Bellomy may wish to consider this before making his next recommendation on air.

A WORD FROM ELIAS

The ordinary things are worth reporting.

Why a town newspaper needs room for the small stories.

Editorial · Elias Mercer

Not every story begins with a mystery. Sometimes it begins with a repaired clock, a remembered coffee order or a neighbour who has noticed that somebody could use a hand.

These things do not always make the loudest headlines. They do, however, make a place feel like home. A newspaper that ignores them has missed something important.

The Cedar Hollow Chronicle will keep space for local businesses, familiar faces, friendly disagreements and the details that make this town itself. We will also keep asking questions. It would be unreasonable to expect otherwise.

If Felix wishes to describe that as being nosy, he is welcome to write a letter to the editor. We suspect he would prefer to announce it.
